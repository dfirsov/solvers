⊢Propositional Logic Solvers

Seven interactive tools for a propositional logic course. Each one takes a formula and works through a method step by step, checking every move rather than just handing over an answer.

⊨

Semantic Tableaux

Test a formula by hunting for a counterexample. Sign the root, click a line, pick the rule its main connective calls for, and close the branches. Every step is validated, and every misstep is explained.

signed T/F tableaux · α and β rules → ∴

Natural Deduction

Grow a proof tree from both ends at once: eliminations run forward out of the assumptions, introductions run backward from the goal, and the proof is finished when the two meet. Discharged assumptions are bracketed and numbered, and the verdict says whether you needed the classical rule.

Gentzen trees · intro and elim rules → ⇒

Sequent Calculus

Gentzen's LK, read bottom-up. Left and right rules come in symmetric pairs, every rule strictly simplifies the sequent, and a branch that runs out of rules without closing hands you a countermodel — so the search does not just check proofs, it decides validity.

cut-free LK · countermodels → ◇

BDD Builder

Grow a decision diagram slot by slot, splitting on whichever variable you like at each node and stopping as soon as a branch is constant. Share subgraphs to make it a DAG, reduce it, and find out whether any variable order fits what you built.

Shannon expansion · unordered diagrams → ◆

ROBDD Constructor

Fix a variable order up front, then reduce the full decision tree with C1, C2 and C3 until nothing applies. Drag nodes to untangle the diagram, and read the finished ROBDD back out as a formula.

reduction rules · Shannon expansion → ∑

Arithmetic Encoding

Turn a formula into ordinary arithmetic over 0 and 1 — negation becomes 1 − p, conjunction becomes multiplication — then simplify it yourself with the ring laws and x·x = x. Every step is checked, and a tautology ends up as the number 1.

Boolean polynomials · unique normal form → ≡

Equivalence Checker

Decide whether two formulas denote the same Boolean function. When they do not, it names a valuation where they disagree and marks the rows of the truth table that split them.

truth tables · counterexamples →

Notation

All seven read the same syntax: ¬ ∧ ∨ ⊃ ≡, with ⊃ for implication and ≡ for the biconditional. Variables are letters.

If those characters are awkward to type, ~ & | -> <-> work just as well, as do !, /\, \/, => and the arrows → and ↔. Each page also has a row of buttons that insert the symbols for you.
